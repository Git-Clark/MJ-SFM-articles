In First Words: How young Hong Kongers navigate Cantonese, Mandarin and English at first contact

Feb 28, 2026 Guo Qiushi

On a steamy morning during the 2025 intake day, crowds spill from the MTR onto Lingnan University’s hillside walkway, tote bags bumping, lanyards swinging. At a welcome booth, Karina Zeng, 24, asks for directions. “Nei hou,” she begins in Cantonese. The student smiles apologetically, then slides into Mandarin. Karina tries three times, each time opening in a different language and receiving a different one back. “Rule of three,” she says. “How can I be wrong three times?”

In recent years, a wave of young arrivals has put new pressure on Hong Kong’s first-contact language etiquette. This shift plays out on campuses and in entry-level offices, where the opening choice, Cantonese, Mandarin, or English, is renegotiated in seconds. One group feeling this shift is the Shenzhen-Hong Kong cross-border students (CBS), who live in Shenzhen and study in Hong Kong, as anlayzed in a 2024 journal article. Their two-city routines shape language habits and identity.

Karina, a CBS student, says she reads cues: signage, age, accent, yet still treats the first greeting as “a guess.” When the reply lands in another language, she simply mirrors it to keep the conversation moving.

Beyond campuses, the same pattern shows up at work. Hong Kong expanded imported labor from what used to be about 3,100 workers a year to more than 71,000 workers over an 18-month period in 2023-2024, a 23-fold surge, according to a Hong Kong government report. The influx puts more strangers, and more languages, in first contact.

In a finance office in Central, Amy Liu, 26, sees the same pattern. “People choose to talk in the most comfortable language,” said Ms. Liu, a mainland returnee who studied in Canada and now works at a local bank. “Day to day, Cantonese dominates. Mandarin comes up more than I expected with non-local clients and younger colleagues. English is there for emails and meetings, but not for coffee chats.”

Applied linguist Dr. Nick Wong of the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology say English surfaces most in professional settings for functional tasks. Younger speakers “generally find switching less awkward,” he said, adding that he avoids mixing codes with many older people unless invited. “Among students and early-career staff you hear rapid accommodation, they listen to the first reply and align quickly.”

According to the Census and Statistics Department (2024), 92.3% of residents reported Cantonese as the language most often used at home, 2.1% English, and 2.1% Putonghua.

Rule of three. How can I be wrong three times?

“To my surprise, many local colleagues aren’t that fluent in English,” said Ms. Liu. “Cantonese is crucial if you want to fit in. English is fine just for working, but to build deeper ties you need Cantonese; it helps you be included in the circle.”

Dr. Wong draws a line between bilingual and multilingual. Truly balanced bilinguals are rare; Hong Kong is better described as a multilingual society. People may not use any two languages equally well, but most are exposed to several. That unevenness makes first-contact choices more variable and raises the odds of an initial mismatch.

Taken together, the interviews point to fit, not status. Cantonese still grounds daily life: Mandarin is rising with mainland classmates and younger hires; English holds for formal conversations. In a city as multilingual as Hong Kong, the first word doesn’t have to be perfect. You guess, you mirror, you keep going, residents say. That fast, flexible shuffle is what makes Hong Kong’s first-contact etiquette unique. The “wrong” start may be just the first step to the right conversation.
