Healing in the dark: emerging adults are paying for sleepovers

Jan 20, 2026 Shuoyi Chen

25-year-old Song Jingyi never thought she would wake up in a zoo, with the memory of being startled by a lynx vigorously tapping on the glass window remaining vividly in her mind. It is now 8 a.m. at Nanjing Hongshan Forest Zoo, and everyone seemed slightly fatigued yet the excitement from last night’s adventure has not faded.

Unlike daytime demeanor that permeated with a distinct “office stench,” the behind-the-scenes lives of animals are brimming with delightful surprises. Some timid creatures will choose to engage in activities at night, and, of course, you may also catch a porcupine family enjoying their late-night snack or a giraffe staying up late.

The duet of wolf howls and the chorus from the monkey hill is a truly unique lullaby.

“The ‘duet’ of wolf howls and the chorus from the monkey hill is a truly unique lullaby,” said Song Jingyi, who is about to complete her doctorate.

In the era of post-pandemic, a growing number of tourist attractions in China, particularly zoos, museums, and aquariums, have begun offering sleepover projects. Originally designed as family-focused study tours, it burnt out of the concept “learning beyond the classroom” and became a splash of color in the development of China’s tourism market.

The niche features of travel-off-peak and immersive experience drove many emerging adults to pay for extra emotional value. Take sleepover at museum as example, data from several leading online travel agencies indicates that online guides for these events have doubled since July.

Although most sleepover-night projects usually follow the same pattern such as a night tour, explainer activities, interactive games, and then backing to tents or dormitories, venues matter when it comes to specific content. “No one really wants to spend the night sleeping in a gallery full of mummies,” Gamila said, who has joined overnight events at both the British Museum and the Zhuhai Chimelong Ocean Kingdom in China. “Besides, room and board in China leave London in the dust,” Gamila added.

There exists an inherent disconnect when adults join programs tailored for children. According to information from the ticket office of “Duo Yang Xing Qiu” institution, even as an early mover in organizing zoo sleepovers for adults, details of programs hardly differ from children’s to adult’s.

Yet for some youngsters, such childlike experience is precisely what they are looking for. “The playful tone of the guide, and the way we enjoyed this tour made me feel like childhood and that’s healing,” shared Liu Qingqing, a law student from Shenzhen University.

Commenting on this trend, Huang Lili, with three years of experience in the in-depth study tour sector, still believes tailored design for diverse audiences is the future: “No one would refuse being treated with greater care.”
